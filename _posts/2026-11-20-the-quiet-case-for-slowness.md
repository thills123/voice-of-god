Every tool I use is faster than the version it replaced, and I am not convinced
I am thinking any better for it. The editor opens instantly, the search returns
in nine milliseconds, the draft autosaves before I have finished the sentence.
And yet the sentences themselves take exactly as long as they always did.

<!--more-->

There is a particular confidence that comes from a fast machine. It suggests
that the work is moving, that the queue is draining, that progress is being
made. Most of that feeling is about the machine. Very little of it is about the
work.

## The cost of never waiting

Waiting used to be built into the process. You wrote something, printed it,
left it on a desk, and read it the next morning with slightly different eyes.
The delay was not an inefficiency. It was the part of the process where you
noticed what you had actually said.

Now the delay has been optimised away, and with it a small but reliable source
of judgement. We publish at the speed of typing and then wonder why so much of
what we publish reads like typing.

> A draft that has never been left alone is not really a draft. It is a
> transcript of your first opinion.

## What slowness actually buys

I do not mean that everything should be done slowly. Some things should be done
quickly and thrown away. The distinction matters:

- **Decisions** benefit from a night's sleep, because the cost of being wrong is
  usually larger than the cost of being late.
- **Drafts** benefit from distance, because you cannot edit what you still
  believe.
- **Tools** should be fast, so that the slowness can live where it belongs:
  in the thinking.

The mistake is letting the speed of the tool set the tempo of the judgement.
A fast typewriter does not make a faster writer. It only makes the bad
sentences arrive sooner.

## A small practice

The practice I have settled on is unglamorous. I write in a plain text file,
save it, and close it. If the idea still seems worth the trouble the next
morning, it gets another pass. If it does not, the file stays closed and nobody
is the poorer for it.

Two rules keep it honest. First, no publishing on the same day as writing.
Second, no counting drafts as output. The second rule is harder, because a
draft feels like an accomplishment, and closing the laptop feels like a
surrender.

But the work that survives is rarely the work that arrived fastest. It is the
work that was given enough time to become something other than a reflex.
